# 3. Tuning

Tuning is a short list of settings, tested one at a time, in an order that keeps you from chasing the
wrong one. Every value below was measured on this machine, not copied from a profile. The ladder is about
two hours of printing: stop after step 3 for a usable printer, and the rest removes the last flaws.

## Order matters

```
retraction  ->  flow  ->  temperature  ->  acceleration  ->  pressure advance
```

- **Retraction first.** Stringing and blobs hide everything else.
- **Flow second.** Get it wrong and every later test measures a wall that is too thick or too thin.
- **Temperature third.** It changes the melt, which changes what flow and acceleration look like.
- **Acceleration fourth.** The big lever here. Settle it before judging the fine stuff.
- **Pressure advance last.** The weakest lever here. Measure it at the acceleration you actually run.

Skip around and you will fix the wrong setting twice. You need digital calipers (0.01 mm), the filament
you actually print with, a way to run G-code (Fluidd or Mainsail), and a notebook. Change one thing per
print, and write the setting on the bottom of the print with a marker - photos lie about which band is
which a week later. Every test here is generated by `tools/make_test_gcode.py`, which writes plain
straight moves, so it needs no slicer and no arc support (this printer has none, see `02-config.md`).

## 1. Retraction length

Pulls filament back up the tube so the nozzle stops dripping while it travels. The tube here is about
700 mm, so the filament inside it is long and springy. That is why retraction is 5 mm here and not the
0.8 mm you see on direct-drive printers.

**The test:** two free-standing cylinders, 10 mm across, 40 mm apart, no bridge between them. Five bands
of retraction length, bottom to top: 5.0 / 5.5 / 6.0 / 6.5 / 7.0 mm, 10 layers each.

```
python3 tools/make_test_gcode.py retract-pair --bands 5.0,5.5,6.0,6.5,7.0
```

**Why two cylinders and not a tower:** the gap between them is dry travel. No wall is printed across it,
so any plastic in that gap came out of the nozzle when it should not have. A four-column tower has three
gaps per layer plus a raft, and all that extra travel makes it read worse than the machine is. Retract on
every travel too, brim included: our first version did not, and the "strings" in the photo came from the
brim.

| What you see in the gap | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Clean from some band upward | That band and every band above it is enough |
| Whiskers in every band, including the top | Not enough length, or too fast, or wet filament |
| Clean in every band, including the bottom | You started too high. The minimum is below your lowest band |

**We got 5.0 mm.** All five bands were string-free, including the lowest, so 5.0 mm is enough and the
true minimum is below it - we never bracketed it, because the sweep started at 5.0. **The usual
mistake:** starting the sweep too high and calling the lowest clean band "the minimum". It is not a
minimum, it is just the smallest number you tried.

## 2. Retraction speed

How fast the filament is pulled back. Too slow and the ooze happens before the pull finishes; too fast
and the extruder grinds a flat spot into the filament, which then slips.

**The test:** same two cylinders, same length, one speed per band: 30 / 40 / 45 / 50 / 60 mm/s. Clean
gap and no noise means good. A click at every retraction, with the gap getting *worse* rather than
better, means grinding. Stringing at every speed even at 5 mm is not a speed problem: wet filament.

**We got 45 mm/s.** Honest note: we did not sweep speed. 45 mm/s was clean on the first print and
stayed, comfortably below the grinding range for a 2.85 mm Bowden feed. If your extruder clicks on
retraction, this is the first number to lower.

**The usual mistake:** raising speed to fight stringing. Stringing is mostly a length and wet-filament
problem, and past a point more speed just makes the extruder slip, which looks like under-extrusion.

## 3. Pressure advance

> **Run this last.** It is third here because it is the number everyone looks for first. Do the other
> steps before it, and check it at the acceleration you settle on.

**In one sentence:** pressure advance compensates for the springiness of the filament in the tube, by
pushing a little extra plastic early and pulling some back late, so the pressure at the nozzle matches
what the slicer asked for. Klipper's notes: <https://www.klipper3d.org/Pressure_Advance.html>.

That works well on direct-drive printers, where the tube is short and stiff. Here the tube is long and
soft and the effect is smeared out - that is the whole story of this setting on this machine.

**The test:** a 20 x 20 mm cube, 18 mm tall, 2 walls, one value per band, at the acceleration you
intend to run.

```
python3 tools/make_test_gcode.py pa-cube --start 0.00 --stop 0.80 --step 0.10 --accel 3000
```

| What you see | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Corner bulges outward, wall starts fat and blobby | Too low. Too much pressure stored when the nozzle arrives |
| Corner sharp, wall even, no gap after the corner | Right |
| Corner pulled inward, thin or gappy wall just after it | Too high. Plastic was pulled back that the corner needed |

**We got:** at 3000 mm/s², bulged at 0.00-0.20, **clean 0.30-0.40**, pulled in 0.50-0.80. At
7000 mm/s², **0.20-0.92 all looked the same**. We kept **0.28** — between the 0.20 and 0.30 bands; the clean window at 3000 is roughly 0.28-0.44.
Nothing argued for another value, and changing a working number on no evidence is how a machine
ends up untunable. **The usual mistake:** blaming corner bulges on pressure advance. That is exactly what we did;
the bulges survived a proven-useless sweep across a 5x range, because the cause was acceleration
(section 6). If a bulge does not move when you sweep this, stop sweeping.

## 4. Flow

A multiplier on how much plastic is pushed per move. `1.0` means "believe the slicer". Anything else is
a fudge factor, and fudge factors hide broken settings.

**The test:** a single-wall cube, one 0.45 mm wall, 20 mm tall, no infill. Measure the wall with
calipers, four places, **mid-height**. Use two cubes: a single thin wall collapses if a layer takes less
than about 4 seconds, and the second cube doubles the layer time.

```
python3 tools/make_test_gcode.py flow-cube --wall 0.45 --speed 40
```

Your target is the line width you asked for, 0.45 mm - not "the number on the box".

| Measured wall | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0.45 - 0.48 mm | Correct | Leave flow at 1.0 |
| Above 0.50 mm | Over-extruding | Lower flow, but find out why first |
| Below 0.43 mm | Under-extruding | Check filament diameter and rotation_distance first |

**We got 0.40 mm**, so **flow stays 1.0**; if anything the machine is slightly under. Our reading was at
the top rim, which reads thin because the last bead has no layer pressing on it. **The usual mistake:**
using flow to fix a shape problem. This machine once ran at flow 1.176, compensating for a wrong
`rotation_distance` (the number telling Klipper how far the motor turns per mm of filament). Once that
was measured properly, 1.176 was wrong twice over. Flow is not a fix for anything except flow.

## 5. Temperature

How hot the plastic is when it lands. Hotter flows and sticks better; cooler sags and strings less.

**The test:** one cube in 2 mm bands, 220 down to 195 °C. Speed, acceleration and pressure advance stay
identical - only the temperature changes.

```
python3 tools/make_test_gcode.py temp-cube --temps 220,215,210,205,200,195
```

Read the bands for surface finish and layer adhesion, then bend the cube: the band that cracks first is
too cold.

**We got 220 °C, because the test showed no difference.** The read was "almost no difference" between
220 and 195. With nothing to measure we kept the hot end of the range: more flow headroom before the
extruder runs out of melt, and better layer bonding. **The usual mistakes:** printing cold to fight
stringing, which only makes weak, under-extruded parts - and a test-design one: **retract and park off the
part before every temperature wait.** A nozzle that sits at the part edge during `M109` leaves a blob
there, and you spend an hour reading blobs your own test file created.

## 6. Acceleration

The one that mattered here. If you read one section, read this one.

Acceleration (mm/s²) is how fast the head may change speed. The head cannot take a corner at full speed:
it slows to a small corner speed (5 mm/s on this machine), goes round, and speeds back up. How long that
takes is set by acceleration - and while the head crawls, the nozzle is still under pressure and still
laying plastic. That extra plastic lands in the corner as a bulge. Going from 5 mm/s back up to 60 mm/s
takes:

| Acceleration | Time spent slow, both sides of one corner |
|---|---|
| 500 mm/s² | about 220 ms |
| 1500 mm/s² | about 73 ms |
| 3000 mm/s² | about 37 ms |
| 7000 mm/s² | about 16 ms |

At 500 mm/s² the nozzle creeps around every corner for a fifth of a second, which is a long time to
ooze into one spot. At 7000 it is over in a blink.

**The test:** one cube, bands of different acceleration, constant speed and constant pressure advance.

```
python3 tools/make_test_gcode.py accel-cube --accels 500,1500,3000,5000,7000 --speed 60
```

Run a fingernail up each corner and look along it under a light, and watch for **ringing** (faint echoes
on the wall just after a corner) and a **layer shift** (a band offset sideways from lost steps) - those
are your signal that you went too far.

| What you see | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bulged in the low bands, cleaner higher up | Dwell ooze. Raise acceleration |
| Bulged in every band, including the highest | Not acceleration. Go back to flow (section 4) |
| Clean corners but ringing or a layer shift in the top band | Too far. Drop one step |

**We got 7000 mm/s².** The lows (500, 1500) were the bad ones; 5000 and 7000 were visibly cleaner, sides
measuring 20.2 mm with sharp corners. This reversed our expectation - we had assumed the frame could not
take high acceleration. It was not the frame. The machine was simply being asked to crawl.

**The nasty mistake:** your slicer can pin acceleration without telling you. Ours emitted
`M204 P1500 R1500 T1500` in its start G-code. Klipper takes the lower of `P` and `T`, so every
print ran at 1500 mm/s² whatever `printer.cfg` said. (A bare `M204 P1500` does nothing —
Klipper needs `S`, or both `P` and `T`.) That is why the same bulges appeared on print after print. Check with
`grep -n "M204\|M201\|M203" your-file.gcode`, remove what comes back, or set acceleration in the slicer
profile. Then confirm the running value on the machine: ask Klipper for `toolhead.max_accel`, or read the
`SET_VELOCITY_LIMIT` line as the print starts (<https://www.klipper3d.org/Slicers.html>). Then
**re-check pressure advance** at the new acceleration.

## 7. Overhangs

Not a setting. This is a limit you measure, then design around.

**The test:** print a standard overhang model and find the angle where the underside stops being clean.
Use a real one, sliced normally: "Overhang Test" by tomi3d on Printables
(<https://www.printables.com/model/126029-overhang-test>, CC BY-NC 4.0) steps through the angles in one
print. The last good step is your ceiling at full fan: below it prints well, above it needs support.

**We got a number.** On this machine, at 220 C with the single radial fan at 100 percent, the arms
printed cleanly up to about **55 degrees from vertical**. Past that the underside went rough and the
tips sagged; at 50-60 it is visibly out of range. **Design at 45 degrees** - it leaves margin for a
colder or wetter day, and for a part that actually matters.

How we got there is worth knowing, because we did it wrong first. We hand-built an overhang test
twice and both failed - not the printer, the geometry we wrote. Version one started each arm with a
flat 5 mm bridge, an extreme overhang at any angle, so every arm collapsed on its first layer.
Version two grew the arms correctly but hung a 10 mm shelf off a single 0.45 mm bead with no brim,
far too fragile, and the nozzle dragged the curled edge. Use the standard model and spend your time
on the reading, not on the geometry.
**The usual mistake:** writing your own overhang test. The geometry is harder than it looks, and a
fragile test fails for reasons that have nothing to do with the printer.

## 8. Maximum volumetric flow

The ceiling on how much plastic can leave the nozzle per second (mm³/s). Above it the extruder cannot keep up.

Estimate what you use now: **line width x layer height x speed**. At 0.45 mm, 0.2 mm layers and 60 mm/s
that is **5.4 mm³/s**. That is low, so this is not what limits the machine - acceleration and the hot end
are.

**To find the ceiling:** print a tower that steps speed up every few millimetres at fixed temperature and
layer height, and find the band where extrusion thins, the surface goes rough, or the extruder clicks.
Convert that band's speed with the formula above; Orca Slicer has a calibration for this. **We have not
measured it.** PLA at 220 °C is often quoted at 10-15 mm³/s, but that is a general figure, not a
measurement of this machine. **The usual mistake:** raising the ceiling to print faster before
acceleration is sorted - then the extruder cannot keep up mid-print and you spend a day blaming flow.

## The numbers we ended with

| Setting | Value | How we know |
|---|---|---|
| Retraction length | 5.0 mm | No stringing at 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5 or 7.0 mm |
| Retraction speed | 45 mm/s | Clean first try, not swept |
| Pressure advance | 0.28 | Clean 0.30-0.40 at 3000 mm/s²; no visible effect 0.20-0.92 at 7000 |
| Flow | 1.0 | Single wall 0.40 mm against a 0.45 mm target |
| Nozzle temperature | 220 °C | No visible difference 195-220 °C |
| Bed temperature | 60 °C | First layer sticks, no warping |
| Acceleration | 7000 mm/s² | 5000-7000 visibly cleaner than 500-1500 |
| Max volumetric flow | not measured | Running at 5.4 mm³/s, well under any ceiling |

`02-config.md` shows where these go in `printer.cfg`. The evidence behind each number, including the test
that failed twice, is in `07-results.md`. Symptoms and fixes are in `06-troubleshooting.md`.

One rule above all: change one thing, print one test, write down what you saw. The printer is not
mysterious - it is a machine with eight numbers, and you can only find them one at a time.
